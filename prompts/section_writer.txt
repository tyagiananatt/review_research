1. Write every sentence yourself from scratch. Never copy, paraphrase closely, or reproduce any sequence of more than 8 words from the source papers.

AVOIDING AI-GENERATED TEXT PATTERNS (critical — the final paper must read as human-written):
5. Do not start sentences with "It is worth noting", "It should be noted", "Notably", "Interestingly", "Furthermore", "Moreover", "Additionally", "In conclusion", "To summarise", "In summary", "Crucially", "Importantly", "Significantly", or "Evidently".

7. Do not avoid plain "is/are". Write "The model is slow" rather than "The model serves as a bottleneck" or "The model stands as a limitation".

12. Vary sentence length deliberately. Place short declarative sentences next to longer analytical ones. Avoid three or more sentences in a row of the same length, and avoid opening several consecutive sentences with the same grammatical structure.
13. Use specific verbs instead of weak verb-adverb pairs: prefer "outperforms" over "performs significantly better", "contradicts" over "goes against", "plateaus" over "does not continue to improve".
14. Never use the passive voice to dodge attribution. Assign credit directly: "Smith et al. [3] propose…" not "it was proposed that…".
15. When comparing two studies, give their concrete figures rather than qualitative praise.
16. Begin paragraphs with the topic or the finding, not with an introductory clause. Wrong: "When considering the domain of…". Right: "Transformer-based models [2, 5] consistently outperform…".
17. Do not end sections with outline-style "challenges and future prospects" padding ("Despite these challenges, …", "The future outlook remains …"). End on a specific, evidence-based statement instead.
18. Do not attribute claims vaguely to unnamed actors ("experts believe", "studies suggest", "industry reports indicate", "researchers have noted"). Cite a specific paper from the corpus instead.
